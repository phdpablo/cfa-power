Abstract

This notebook presents a pedagogical tutorial on statistical power analysis in Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Proper planning of sample sizes requires assessing the power to detect overall model misfit by analytical approach or simulation-based approach. Standard analytical tools typically rely on asymptotic normal theory, which may break down when confronted with non-normality or missing data. This tutorial demonstrates both analytical strategies (using semPower and semTools) and Monte Carlo simulations (using simsem) side-by-side using the WHOQOL-BREF 4-factor model as a unifying case study. We illustrate how simulation can complement analytical calculations, allowing researchers to evaluate parameter-level power, coverage, and relative bias under realistic scenarios involving varying degrees of model misspecification, missing data, and continuous non-normal indicators. Ultimately, we argue that analytical methods are ideal for initial planning, while simulations constitute the gold standard for robust confirmation.

1 Introduction

Statistical power in Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) reflects the probability of correctly rejecting an incorrectly specified model, or detecting a parameter of interest when it is truly non-zero in the population (MacCallum et al., 1996). Power analysis is critical when planning a CFA study; however, researchers often rely on arbitrary rules of thumb mapping required sample sizes to the number of measured variables or parameters, rather than formal prospective calculations.

There are two main paradigms to power analysis in CFA:

  1. Analytical methods: Rely on asymptotic statistical theory (e.g., the non-central \(\chi^2\) distribution) to calculate the required sample size for detecting global/local model misfit based on established fit indices like the RMSEA (Moshagen & Bader, 2024).
  2. Simulation-based methods: Use Monte Carlo data generation to repeatedly establish how the model behaves under assumed population models, allowing the estimation of power, bias, and coverage for specific parameters or overall fit indices across varying sample sizes (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).

This document provides a comprehensive tutorial covering analytical methods using the semPower package, and Monte Carlo methods using the simsem package.

NoteReproducibility Note

The results throughout this tutorial will thus show more sampling variability than you would expect in a final study. All analytical and simulation parameters are centrally defined in helpers.R and models.R in Scripts/DataAppendixScripts.

2 The Running Example: WHOQOL-BREF Population CFA Model

To anchor our power analyses, we use the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) 4-factor measurement instrument. Meaningful power analysis requires the definition of a population model—the “true” state of the world from which we believe our data are generated. By simulating from a meta-analytically derived population model rather than a purely arbitrary one, researchers can ensure that tests reflect realistic boundaries (Lin & Yao, 2022; Mosqueira Taipe et al., 2026).

We specify four population variations to perform sensitivity analyses:

  1. Meta-analytic: Loadings and residual variances closely matching Lin & Yao (2022).
  2. Naive: A pessimistic baseline where factor loadings are based on the lowest reported reliability values per domain (Mosqueira Taipe et al., 2026).
  3. Optimistic: A best-case scenario where factor loadings are based on the highest reported reliability values per domain (Mosqueira Taipe et al., 2026).
  4. H1: A misspecified model with three cross-loadings based on the meta-EFA results from Lin & Yao (2022).

2.1 Population model

The primary population model represents a meta-analytic scenario based on Lin & Yao (2022). Factor loadings (\(\lambda\)) match the pooled empirical Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) estimates across 16 international studies. Characteristics:

  • Interfactor correlations (\(\phi\)): Set to \(0.20\) (conservative midpoint of the reported range \(0.08\)–\(0.31\)).
  • Residual covariance: \(Q3\) and \(Q4\) share a residual covariance (\(\text{r}(Q3, Q4 | \text{physical}) = 0.30\)), maintaining the subfactor signal observed in the EFA 5-factor solution (Lin & Yao, 2022).
  • Factor structure: Item \(Q8\) is maintained in the environment domain despite having a higher cross-loading on the psychological domain in the meta-EFA (\(\lambda_{\text{psy}} > \lambda_{\text{env}}\)). This represents the null hypothesis (\(H_0\)) version of the population, strictly maintaining the original 4-factor structure without cross-loadings.

2.2 Naive model

This model defines a pessimistic, empirically grounded scenario. We apply the Spearman-Brown prediction formula to derive tau-equivalent factor loadings based on the lowest reported Cronbach’s alpha (\(\alpha\)) values.

  • Approach: Assuming equal loadings within each domain, we estimate the loadings as \(\lambda = \sqrt{\alpha / (k - \alpha(k-1))}\) and residual variances as \(\theta = 1 - \lambda^2\).
  • Alpha sources: Based on the lowest alphas for the original 4-factor structure reported by a single study identified in the systematic review by Mosqueira Taipe et al. (2026) (Physical \(\alpha = 0.672\); Psychological \(\alpha = 0.704\); Social \(\alpha = 0.630\); Environmental \(\alpha = 0.709\)). All values reside within the \(M - 1SD\) range reported by Lin & Yao (2022).
  • Interfactor correlations (\(\phi\)): Fixed at \(0.30\) (the upper bound from Lin & Yao (2022)), which makes factors harder to distinguish and yields a conservative power estimate.
  • Derived parameters: E.g., Physical (\(\lambda = 0.4758, \theta = 0.7736\)), Psychological (\(\lambda = 0.5328, \theta = 0.7161\)), Social (\(\lambda = 0.6017, \theta = 0.6379\)), Environmental (\(\lambda = 0.4832, \theta = 0.7665\)). The residual covariance between \(Q3\) and \(Q4\) is adjusted to \(0.2321\).

2.3 Optimistic model

This model defines an optimistic, empirically grounded scenario using the same Spearman-Brown derivation approach but leveraging the highest observed Cronbach’s alpha (\(\alpha\)) values per domain.

  • Alpha sources: Based on maximum alphas reported in the literature (Mosqueira Taipe et al., 2026) (Physical \(\alpha = 0.860\); Psychological \(\alpha = 0.860\); Social \(\alpha = 0.810\); Environmental \(\alpha = 0.849\)). All values are confirmed within the \(M + 1SD\) range from Lin & Yao (2022).
  • Interfactor correlations (\(\phi\)): Fixed at \(0.08\) (the lower bound from Lin & Yao (2022)), resulting in factors that are well-distinguished and providing an optimistic power estimate.
  • Derived parameters: E.g., Physical (\(\lambda = 0.6837, \theta = 0.5326\)), Psychological (\(\lambda = 0.7113, \theta = 0.4941\)), Social (\(\lambda = 0.7661, \theta = 0.4130\)), Environmental (\(\lambda = 0.6424, \theta = 0.5873\)). 2.4 Misspecification model

This represents the alternative hypothesis (\(H_1\)), defining a cross-loading population model. Three empirical cross-loadings are added to the baseline 4-factor structure based on Lin & Yao (2022) and supported by recent literature (Mosqueira Taipe et al., 2026):

  • Cross-loading 1: item \(Q8\) loading on the Psychological factor (\(\lambda = 0.55\)). This item showed the highest betweenness (\(40.50\)) and explicitly grouped with the psychological factor in 9/16 studies (Lin & Yao, 2022).
  • Cross-loading 2: item \(Q9\) loading on the Psychological factor (\(\lambda = 0.21\)). Identified as a secondary loading in the 5-factor solution.
  • Cross-loading 3: item \(Q15\) loading on the Environmental factor (\(\lambda = 0.24\)). “Mobility” conceptually bridges physical ability and environmental access, showing the second-highest betweenness (\(36.50\)) (Lin & Yao, 2022).

Residual variances (\(\theta\)) for cross-loaded items were recalculated to preserve total item variance using the formula \(\theta = 1 - \lambda_{\text{primary}}^2 - \lambda_{\text{secondary}}^2 - 2 \lambda_{\text{primary}} \lambda_{\text{secondary}} \phi\), where the interfactor correlation (\(\phi\)) is \(0.20\).

2.6 Analysis model

The analysis model serves dual purposes depending on the simulation paradigm:

  • Misspecification detection: Acts as the null hypothesis (\(H_0\)), meaning it omits the cross-loadings, when fitting data generated from the misspecification model (\(H_1\)). Here, power is the probability of correctly rejecting the model given that cross-loadings exist in the population.
  • Recovery power: Estimates the 4-factor structure plus the \(Q3\) and \(Q4\) residual covariance given data from the meta-analytic, naive, or optimistic population models.

Key constraints and features:

  • Identification: Factor variances are fixed to \(1\) (marker-free scaling) allowing the first indicator to be freely estimated.
  • Residual covariance: \(Q3\) and \(Q4\) correlation is freely estimated, which is theoretically justified and empirically supported (Lin & Yao, 2022; Mosqueira Taipe et al., 2026).
  • Cross-loadings: The omission of cross-loadings for items \(Q8\), \(Q9\), and \(Q15\) deliberately defines the misspecification tested in misspecification detection.

3 Analytical Power Analysis

Analytical power usually asks: “How large must \(N\) be to reject the hypothesis that the model fits perfectly (or adequately) when the true misfit in the population is of a certain magnitude?” A common formulation sets the alternative hypothesis to RMSEA = 0.05 (MacCallum et al., 1996).

3.1 Model Degrees of Freedom

Analytical calculations depend crucially on model degrees of freedom (\(df\)), the difference between the number of available pieces of information (unique variances/covariances) and the number of freely estimated parameters. Knowing this allows tools to parameterize the non-central \(\chi^2\) distribution. This calculation is necessary for both a priori and post hoc power analyses in analytical methods for global fit:

3.2 A Priori: How Many Observations?

3.2.1 Model-free approach (RMSEA-based)

An a priori power analysis is conducted before data collection to determine the sample size required to detect a target effect with a prespecified significance level, desired power, and model degrees of freedom (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

In this setup, the effect is defined through the RMSEA, which expresses model misfit on a per-degree-of-freedom basis and therefore serves as a noncentrality-based measure of discrepancy between the null model and the comparison model (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

Because RMSEA is directly linked to the population minimum of the fitting function through \(F_0 = \text{RMSEA}^2 \times df\), the target misfit can be translated into the noncentrality parameter used in the analytical power calculation, \(\lambda = (N - 1)F_0\) (Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

This corresponds to an analytical, model-free power analysis, meaning that the required sample size is determined by the chosen effect magnitude, \(\alpha\), desired power, and \(df\), rather than by the interpretation of any single model parameter (Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

Accordingly, an RMSEA-based a priori analysis is most appropriate when the objective is to plan sample size for detecting overall model misspecification in a global chi-square test of model fit (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

3.2.2 Model-based approach

By contrast, when the substantive hypothesis concerns a specific loading, regression coefficient, factor correlation, or cross-group constraint, a model-based power analysis is typically more appropriate than a global RMSEA-based specification (Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

In this framework, power is defined for a specific hypothesis, so the relevant sample size depends on the discrepancy between the null model and the comparison model rather than on a universal rule of thumb for SEM sample sizes (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

Using semPower.powerLav(), the population model represents the data-generating structure, whereas modelH0 represents the null analysis model to be challenged. When modelH1 is omitted, the null model is evaluated against the saturated model, which makes the analysis a test of global model misfit rather than a test of a single isolated parameter restriction (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

Conceptually, the analytical procedure fits the null model to the population-implied covariance structure and converts the resulting discrepancy into a noncentrality-based effect size. The key quantity is the population minimum of the fitting function, \(F_0\), which is linked to the noncentrality parameter by \(\lambda = (N - 1)F_0\) and therefore connects substantive model misspecification to statistical power (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

Because the effect is generated by the full model specification, the required sample size is inherently model-dependent and may change when loadings, factor correlations, residual variances, or other population parameters are modified (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

This type of analysis is especially useful when the goal is to justify sample size planning from a theoretically specified SEM rather than from a generic fit index threshold. At the same time, its interpretation relies on the assumptions underlying analytical chi-square-based power analysis, so simulation-based approaches become preferable when nonnormality, missing data, or convergence issues are central concerns (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

ImportantLimitations of the Analytical Approach

The RMSEA/\(\chi^2\)-based analytical method classically assumes continuous, multivariate normal data and Maximum Likelihood estimation. 4 Monte Carlo Simulation: Ideal Conditions

This section presents a Monte Carlo framework for evaluating fit-based power in SEM under ideal conditions, that is, under a scenario in which the analyst fully specifies both the reference population model and the alternative population model before data are generated (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Jobst et al., 2023).

The central idea is to distinguish between two roles played by models in the simulation design: a data-generating model and an analysis model (Feng & Hancock, 2023). The data-generating model defines the population from which replicated samples are drawn, whereas the analysis model defines the structure that is repeatedly fit to each generated sample. This separation is essential because statistical power in SEM depends not only on sample size, but also on the discrepancy between the model used for estimation and the population structure that generated the data (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Jobst et al., 2023).

In this setup, the reference condition represents the benchmark of acceptable fit, while the alternative condition represents a population in which a theoretically relevant misspecification is present (Feng & Hancock, 2023). Repeatedly fitting the same analysis model to samples generated under both conditions makes it possible to compare their empirical fit-index distributions and quantify how well the chosen fit criteria separate well-fitting from misspecified situations (Jobst et al., 2023).

Varying \(N\) across a predefined sequence turns the simulation into a sample-size planning exercise rather than a single-condition power check (Feng & Hancock, 2023). Conceptually, as \(N\) increases, the sampling distributions under the reference and alternative conditions tend to become more distinguishable, so the probability of correctly flagging the misspecified condition increases as well (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Jobst et al., 2023).

Power based on fit indices is therefore interpreted here as the probability that a fit rule, calibrated from the reference condition, classifies data generated under the alternative condition as showing worse fit than expected under the benchmark model. In practical terms, this approach shifts the focus from testing a single parameter to evaluating whether global fit measures are sensitive enough to detect a substantively meaningful form of model misspecification across plausible sample sizes (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

This logic is especially useful when the main research question concerns model adequacy at the global level, or when the inferential goal is to determine the sample size required for fit indices to reliably distinguish acceptable from unacceptable model-data correspondence under explicitly defined assumptions (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Jobst et al., 2023).

4.1 Traditional cutoff values for fit indices

When traditional cutoffs are used, the analysis adopts fixed threshold values such as \(\text{RMSEA} \le .06\), \(\text{CFI} \ge .95\), \(\text{TLI} \ge .95\), and \(\text{SRMR} \le .06\) as the decision boundaries for acceptable fit. Conceptually, this means that power is defined here as the proportion of replications in which the misspecified model crosses those predefined boundaries in the direction of worse fit.

This setup is convenient because it mirrors common applied practice, but the literature also emphasizes that such rule-of-thumb cutoffs are not universally valid and should be interpreted cautiously, since fit measures are influenced by model features beyond the focal misspecification itself (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

A simulation-based power display is especially informative in SEM because it shows how the sampling behavior of fit indices changes as sample size varies, rather than relying only on a single summary value (Moshagen & Bader, 2024). This is important because the same nominal cutoff may correspond to different practical rejection behavior depending on the model structure and the empirical distribution of the index under misspecification (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

For that reason, plotting fit-index-based power provides an intuitive bridge between formal power analysis and the applied decision rules researchers often use when judging model adequacy in substantive SEM work (Jobst et al., 2023).

4.2 Targeting specific parameters

The previous analyses focused on global fit indices, which summarize overall model adequacy but do not directly address the power to detect specific parameter misspecifications. In this section, we evaluate power for a particular parameter (e.g., \(Q8\) on the Physical factor) by defining the alternative condition as a population in which that parameter is set to zero, while the reference condition retains the original nonzero parameter. This approach allows us to assess the sensitivity of the analysis to detect a specific parameter misspecification rather than relying on global fit measures alone.

From this perspective, Monte Carlo summaries such as bias, standard error bias, and confidence-interval coverage are not secondary diagnostics. They indicate whether the estimator behaves as expected in finite samples, that is, whether \(\hat{\theta}\) is centered around the population value \(\theta\), whether the estimated uncertainty is calibrated, and whether the nominal interval actually contains the true parameter with approximately the intended frequency across replications (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).

In Table 6, Rel Bias refers to relative parameter bias, Rel SE Bias refers to relative standard-error bias, Coverage is the proportion of confidence intervals that contain the true value, and Power (Not equal 0) is the proportion of replications in which the parameter is detected as different from zero at the chosen significance level (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021). A parameter may show high power and still be poorly recovered if its estimate is biased or its standard error is poorly calibrated, so detection and estimation quality should be evaluated separately (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024; Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).

Overall, the results below are favorable for most parameters, but they also show that power, bias, standard-error bias, and coverage do not tell exactly the same story. The correct interpretation therefore needs to combine all four indicators, rather than relying only on power or only on the required \(N\) (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024; Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).

4.3 Recovering the population parameters

5 Parameter-level power under the naive model

Before turning to global fit, it is useful to examine power from the perspective of parameter recovery. In SEM, power is always tied to a specific hypothesis, so the required sample size may differ substantially depending on whether the focus is on overall model misfit or on individual parameters such as loadings, latent covariances, or residual covariances (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

This subsection therefore uses Monte Carlo simulation to identify the sample size required for each estimable parameter in the CFA model to attain a target power level of $ .80 $. The logic is straightforward: repeated samples are generated under the population model, the analysis model is fit to each sample, and power is summarized for each parameter across candidate values of $ N $ (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).

The purpose of this step is not to evaluate model fit as a whole, but to determine which parameters are easier or harder to recover under the assumed data-generating conditions. This is useful because some parts of a CFA model can be well powered at relatively small sample sizes, whereas others may require substantially larger samples, especially when the focal quantities are covariances or other comparatively subtle effects (Jobst et al., 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

6 Monte Carlo Simulation: Realistic Scenarios

Power analysis in structural equation modeling should be framed as a design-stage procedure that connects substantive expectations, model specification, and data conditions to the probability of obtaining informative statistical tests, rather than as a mechanical search for a single “minimum \(N\)” rule (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Wang, 2023).

SEM power analysis is strongest when it is anchored in a plausible population-generating mechanism rather than in generic rules of thumb or model-free calculators borrowed from simpler analyses (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Kyriazos, 2018).

6.1 Missing Data (MCAR)

Missing data must be treated as part of the design problem, not as a secondary complication to be handled only after estimation, because missingness reduces available information and therefore generally lowers estimation efficiency and statistical power (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Jak et al., 2021).

From this perspective, the relevant question is not only how many cases are planned, but how much usable information remains under the anticipated missing-data process (Feng & Hancock, 2023).

This is one reason Monte Carlo power analysis is especially valuable in SEM with missing data: it allows the analyst to generate data under a prespecified population model, impose realistic missingness mechanisms such as MCAR or MAR, fit the intended analysis model, and estimate power empirically under the same conditions expected in practice (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

Simulation-based power analysis is also advantageous when assumptions underlying purely analytical solutions become less credible, such as in the presence of incomplete data, non-normality, or more complex model structures (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024; Wang, 2023).

In this sense, the Monte Carlo approach is not merely a computational alternative, but a way to align sample size planning with the actual data-generating conditions anticipated for the study (Moshagen & Bader, 2024; Wang, 2023).

Conceptually, the purpose of the analysis is therefore twofold: first, to ensure enough sensitivity to test the focal theoretical relations, and second, to ensure enough sensitivity to evaluate whether the overall model remains substantively interpretable under the planned conditions (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Wang, 2023).

When missing data are expected, this dual objective becomes even more important, because power for parameter detection and power for fit evaluation may deteriorate at different rates as information is lost (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Wang, 2023).

A well-justified SEM power analysis thus rests on explicit assumptions about the population model, the magnitude of focal effects, the measurement structure, the estimation framework, and the expected missing-data mechanism (Jak et al., 2021; Moshagen & Bader, 2024; Wang, 2023).

Under this logic, the reported power results should be understood as conditional on a theoretically motivated scenario, not as universal guarantees, which is precisely why transparent documentation of assumptions is essential in reproducible SEM workflows (Feng & Hancock, 2023; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

6.2 Non-Normality

This section focuses on the condition with mild non-normality, so power should be evaluated using robust fit indices rather than relying on normal-theory fit behavior. When available, the newer robust fit measures should be preferred over older scaled versions because they have better theoretical properties under non-normal data. However, for comparability with the ideal condition, the same set of indices is evaluated here.

The power curves show how often the misspecified model would be rejected as \(N\) changes under this mildly non-normal condition. Here, the rejection rule is not based on generic cutoffs, but on empirical cutoffs obtained from the null-model sampling distribution at the chosen \(\alpha\) level (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).

The version with logistic smoothing emphasizes the general trend of power across sample sizes, whereas the version without smoothing shows the simulated fit-index values more directly (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021). This is useful for checking how clearly the misspecified-model distribution separates from the null-based cutoff as \(N\) increases (Pornprasertmanit et al., 2021).
